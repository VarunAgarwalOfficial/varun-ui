Two knights, one valley

Can two sides ever be sure they agree?

The hook for lecture 1. Two knights camp on either side of a valley. Let the scene sit for a moment before you speak.

They win only if both charge at dawn. One alone loses.

Knight A writes the plan: charge at dawn.

Ask: what does A need to know before charging? Then click: a bee arrives to carry the plan.

A bee carries the plan across. The valley is unsafe: a bee can be lost.

It arrived. But A can't tell that it did.

The bee walks across as the slide opens. Pause on the second line: A's uncertainty is the whole problem.

B sends a bee back: got it.

Lost on the way. Now B can't tell whether A knows.

Click once the bee is mid-valley. It goes. Ask who is unsure now, and of what.

"Why can't they just send a confirmation?"

Good question. Park it: first we need one bigger idea.

Someone always asks this. Don't answer yet. The cart parks the question; we go up a level and come back to it.

Every message can be lost

Any link can drop any message: noise, a full queue, a pulled cable. Silence tells the sender nothing.

A confirmation is a message too. So it can be lost.

The bigger idea, on its own, away from the knights. Click once both bees are out: one goes.

A confirmation is a message, and every message can be lost. Confirming the confirmation only moves the problem one step on.

The cart drives back to the middle. Read the question out, then answer it with the idea from the level above.

How many messages until both knights are sure?

None is enough. The last message can always be lost, and its sender can't tell.

Give the room thirty seconds. Take two answers, then click. The argument: take the shortest plan that works; drop its last message; it must still work; so a shorter plan works. Contradiction.

So no network makes two hosts certain. TCP makes them confident enough: numbered messages, replies and timeouts.

Dawn breaks. Name the three tools; they are the plan for the next four weeks.
